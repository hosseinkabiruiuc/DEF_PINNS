## Abstract

Delayed ettringite formation (DEF) can cause expansion after heat curing and subsequent moisture exposure. This benchmark evaluates reduced kinetic regularization using 61 expansion curves and 1,725 measurements from five studies. The final 20 to 25% of observations was withheld; scaling and model selection used earlier data only. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), data-only networks, direct kinetic fits, persistence, and linear extrapolation were compared. Evidence for a source-wide improvement was weak: the exact five-source test gave p=0.4375 against data-only learning. Delayed first-order PINNs achieved seed-averaged mean curve absolute error of 0.059% strain versus 0.072% for separately tuned data-only learning, an 18.3% reduction. A matched-optimizer ablation gave 0.077% without the kinetic residual. Direct kinetics retained lower pooled error than the seed-mean PINNs, and persistence was more accurate on 41 of 61 curves. Avrami regularization degraded forecasts, and independent tuning preferred zero penalty. The trained networks did not satisfy the kinetic equation: normalized residuals were approximately one. Per-curve scaling reversed the neural ranking. These results support an exploratory comparison of conditional expansion forecasts, not governing-chemistry recovery or susceptibility prediction for unseen mixtures. Effective rate, onset, and capacity must not be interpreted as independently identified reaction parameters.

**Keywords:** delayed ettringite formation; physics-informed neural networks; cementitious materials; expansion kinetics; heat treatment; materials performance

## 1. Introduction

Delayed ettringite formation (DEF) is an internal sulfate-related deterioration mechanism associated with elevated early-age temperature and later moisture exposure [1,2]. Heating can destabilize ettringite and redistribute sulfate among AFm phases and calcium-silicate-hydrate (C-S-H); cooling and resaturation can permit ettringite reformation [3,4]. Expansion may develop after a long latent period [5,6]. The amount of precipitated ettringite is not equivalent to specimen strain: accessible pores can accommodate product, whereas confined growth can generate stress and cracking [7,8]. Thermal duration, cement chemistry, alkalis, supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs), and water-cement ratio affect the response [9,10]. Reinforcement, geometry, and moisture further modify measured length change [11,12]. Consequently, mineralogical evidence of DEF does not uniquely determine the expansion remaining in a structural component [13,14]. Engineering forecasts must account for this distinction between reaction, pore accommodation, and observable deformation.

Existing DEF models range from reduced kinetic relations to coupled chemical and mechanical descriptions [15,16]. Thermodynamic and transport formulations require internal states that retrospective expansion studies rarely report together [16,17]. Reduced models need fewer measurements [18,19], but rate, capacity, and activation time can compensate over a short observed interval. A Larive-type sigmoid supplies a transparent phenomenological comparator, although its origin is alkali-aggregate reaction rather than DEF chemistry [20]. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) combine a learned response with a differential residual [21,22]. Neural ordinary differential equations and universal differential equations offer related approaches [23,24]. Cement applications include hydration and rheology [25,26]. However, a residual penalty does not ensure that the resulting trajectory satisfies its equation. Optimization imbalance and weak residual enforcement can compromise physical interpretation [27,28]. A useful DEF study must therefore compare PINNs with direct kinetics and simple forecasts, measure differential compliance, and separate forecasting accuracy from chemical identification.

Here, we evaluate physics-informed learning of reduced DEF expansion kinetics using source-traceable literature curves. We withhold each curve's final observations and use training-only scaling and nested model selection. Direct kinetic fits, persistence, linear extrapolation, a shape-constrained network, and separately tuned data-only networks provide comparators. Delayed first-order and Avrami residuals test whether additional kinetic structure helps at the available observation density. Curve-balanced errors are the primary endpoint; pooled errors describe the influence of severe misses. Paired comparisons, multiplicity correction, source-clustered resampling, parameter-conditioning diagnostics, and residual-weight sensitivities constrain interpretation. The model learns continuations of already observed series because its primary architecture includes curve identity. The engineering question is whether a reduced kinetic restriction improves expansion forecasts across different processing and exposure histories, rather than whether it predicts susceptibility in an unseen mixture. The contribution is a reproducible benchmark of the utility and limits of kinetic regularization, not a new constitutive theory of DEF.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1 Evidence and Curve Extraction

Literature discovery used Crossref, DOI and publisher links, and open-access and licensed publisher sources. The register contains 9,913 distinct DOIs, with potential title duplicates flagged separately. Of 576 retrieval attempts, 530 yielded unique parseable PDFs; scientific screening retained 74 papers concerning DEF or relevant thermal-curing mechanisms. Five studies supplied the vector-marker trajectories used here. The other 69 retained papers were not quantitatively included; a complete paper-level adjudication of their digitization eligibility is unavailable. This is therefore a curated benchmark rather than an exhaustive systematic review. The screening record follows the transparency principles of PRISMA [29], but does not establish that every eligible published expansion curve was captured. The accompanying files record bibliographic provenance, hashes, screening decisions, and gaps in historical download information.

Kawabata supplied 22 curves [11], and Brunetaud supplied 27 [30]. Aubert and Tiecher supplied four each [31,32]. Martin supplied four [33]. Table 1 summarizes their thermal and exposure conditions. Linear transformations mapped vector marker coordinates extracted from the published PDF figures to time and expansion, with axis-calibration R²≥0.997. The inclusion rule required at least six ordered observations; the realized dataset contained 12 to 60 per curve. Source page, figure, marker coordinates, tick calibration, and verification overlays remain traceable. Independent repeat digitization was not available; coordinate traceability does not establish inter-extractor reliability or error in strain units [34]. One analyst performed scientific screening, so the corpus also retains possible selection bias. Mature-stage heating can produce DEF outside the early-age protocols studied here and remains outside the validated modeling domain [35]. Expansion and absolute errors are reported in % strain; differences between expansion values are percentage points.

Extraction initially yielded 2,410 observations. Thirteen ambiguous Brunetaud marker groups, containing 642 observations, were excluded because branch switching produced a consecutive decrease greater than 0.02 percentage points. This source-specific extraction rule was not applied as a global monotonicity filter. Genuine reversals in other sources were retained. Capping three dense curves at 60 uniformly selected ordered points removed 43 further observations, leaving 61 curves and 1,725 measurements. Nine small negative training values from Martin were retained in the raw data and clipped to zero only in the non-negative modeling field; none was a terminal target. Alternative branch thresholds changed extraction counts but do not establish model robustness without independent branch adjudication.

### 2.2 Reduced Governing Relation

The chemical distinction between conversion and expansion is essential. Sulfate may be released from C-S-H during ettringite reformation [1,3]. In cement notation, C=CaO, A=Al₂O₃, S̄=SO₃, and H=H₂O. An illustrative gypsum end-member balance is

[[EQ: \mathrm{C}_4\mathrm{A}\bar{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{H}_{12}+2\mathrm{C}\bar{\mathrm{S}}\mathrm{H}_2+16\mathrm{H}\rightarrow\mathrm{C}_6\mathrm{A}\bar{\mathrm{S}}_3\mathrm{H}_{32}. | 1]]

Replacing gypsum with anhydrite requires 20H. Phase-volume balances provide chemical bookkeeping rather than a strain law [36,37]; Appendix A distinguishes solid and total reaction volumes without assigning unmeasured phase inventories. Sulfate release from C-S-H changes the reactant assemblage [4], while pore accommodation and restraint determine how product growth contributes to measured expansion.

Let ξ be dimensionless conversion and ξ_{∞} its limiting value. When one reactant is locally in excess, effective first-order depletion gives dξ/dt=k(ξ_{∞}−ξ). This is a reduced closure, not a calibrated multicomponent DEF reaction law [18,19]. For a fixed accommodation threshold ξ_c and a linear observation operator,

[[EQ: \varepsilon=a[\xi-\xi_c]_+,\qquad \varepsilon_{\mathrm{cap}}=a(\xi_\infty-\xi_c). | 2]]

Here a combines reactive volume, strain transfer, and restraint, and [u]₊=max(u,0). After activation, the hard-threshold operator gives dε/dt=k(ε_{cap}−ε). We model activation with a smooth gate:

[[EQ: \frac{\mathrm d\varepsilon}{\mathrm dt}=H_s(t-t_0)k(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cap}}-\varepsilon),\qquad \varepsilon(0)=0. | 3]]

The logistic gate H_s(u)=[1+exp(−su)]⁻¹ is a modeling assumption, not an exact consequence of the hard threshold. The dimensional slope s has units of inverse time; in normalized time it is dimensionless. Define its integrated clock by

[[EQ: \Omega_s(t;t_0)=\frac{\ln[1+\exp(s(t-t_0))]-\ln[1+\exp(-st_0)]}{s}. | 4]]

Since dΩ_s/dt=H_s and Ω_s(0)=0, the exact solution is

[[EQ: \varepsilon(t)=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cap}}\{1-\exp[-k\Omega_s(t;t_0)]\}. | 5]]

In the hard-gate limit, a common preactivation and postactivation rate implies ξ_c/ξ_{∞}=1−exp(−kt₀). That identity fails as a chemical identification if the hidden preactivation rate or moisture history differs. Capacity, rate, and onset are therefore effective expansion descriptors. The model contains no explicit Arrhenius temperature factor, time-dependent moisture term, transport field, crystallization pressure, or damage evolution. Static exposure descriptors do not replace these processes. The data do not support calibrating their coupled governing equations, and the PINNs are not claimed to solve them.

### 2.3 Comparators and Neural Training

Direct first-order, delayed first-order, delayed Avrami, Gompertz, and Larive relations were fitted independently to training observations. Equation (5) defines delayed first order. Ordinary first order uses ε_{cap}[1−exp(−κτ)] without an activation gate. The Avrami form adapts phase-transformation kinetics as an expansion comparator, not a DEF-specific nucleation mechanism [38]. The remaining relations, in normalized time τ, are

[[EQ: \varepsilon_{\mathrm{Av}}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cap}}\{1-\exp[-(\kappa\Omega_s)^n]\},\qquad 1\leq n\leq4. | 6a]]

[[EQ: \varepsilon_{\mathrm G}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cap}}\exp\{-\exp[-\kappa(\tau-\tau_m)]\}. | 6b]]

[[EQ: \varepsilon_{\mathrm L}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cap}}\frac{1-\exp(-\tau/\tau_c)}{1+\exp[-(\tau-\tau_L)/\tau_c]}. | 6c]]

In Eq. (6a), Ω_s is evaluated in normalized time τ with normalized onset τ₀. All direct-fit capacities and time parameters are curve-specific. The Larive form is a phenomenological comparator adapted from ASR, not a DEF-specific chemical law [20]. Unlike the other forms, Gompertz does not enforce zero expansion at the origin. Direct fitting used soft-L1 residuals scaled by max(SD_{train},0.015% strain), with 8, 32, 72, 32, and 32 starts for first order, delayed first order, delayed Avrami, Gompertz, and Larive, respectively. Tolerances were 10⁻¹⁰, and delayed fits used s=200. Bounds were 0≤ε_{cap}≤5%, 10⁻⁴≤κ≤100, 0≤τ₀≤3, −0.5≤τ_m≤3, 10⁻⁴≤τ_c≤10, and 0≤τ_L≤3. The lowest objective across starts was retained, with convergence status reported. Bound contact and the residual-Jacobian condition number diagnose parameter instability. A low-capacity partially pooled delayed first-order comparator shrinks curve-level log parameters toward descriptor/source regressions. This empirical regularization is not a fitted nonlinear mixed-effects population model [39]. Persistence carries the final training measurement forward. Linear extrapolation fits the final five training points and clips negative forecasts to zero.

For curve i, T_i is the final training time. E* is the largest training expansion across all curves, a single global scale. The dimensionless variables are

[[EQ: \tau=t/T_i,\qquad y=\varepsilon/E^*,\qquad \kappa_i=k_iT_i,\qquad \alpha_i=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{cap},i}/E^*. | 7]]

The primary kinetic residual and its Avrami alternative are

[[EQ: \mathcal R_{\mathrm{FO},i}=\partial_\tau y_\theta-H_s(\tau-\tau_{0i})\kappa_i(\alpha_i-y_\theta). | 8a]]

[[EQ: \mathcal R_{\mathrm{Av},i}=\partial_\tau y_\theta-H_s n_i\kappa_i(\kappa_i\Omega_s)^{n_i-1}(\alpha_i-y_\theta). | 8b]]

All terms in Eqs. (7) and (8) are dimensionless. The realized E*=2.0263% strain; T_i ranged from 141.9 to 917.2 d. The integrated clock in Eq. (4) is evaluated in τ with s=200; a 10⁻¹² floor stabilizes fractional powers in the Avrami residual. Eighteen jittered collocation points per curve were regenerated each epoch over the fixed domain 0≤τ≤3. No terminal response or terminal time determined scaling or tuning. A deliberate full-series-scaling sensitivity is reported separately.

The context vector contains an eight-dimensional curve embedding, nine standardized descriptors, and nine missingness indicators. Descriptors cover thermal treatment, storage temperature, water-cement ratio, SO₃/Al₂O₃, alkali, SCM content, moisture, and restraint. Numeric gaps were median-imputed using training information. SO₃/Al₂O₃ was available for only 12 curves. A two-layer, 48-unit tanh head supplies α_i, κ_i, τ_{0i}, and n_i. A three-layer, 64-unit tanh state network receives time, context, and these head outputs; its softplus output enforces non-negativity. Logistic maps constrain 0<α_i<1.5, 0.02<κ_i<30.02, and 1<n_i<4, but do not cap the predicted state. The onset upper bound is the first training crossing of 0.02% plus 0.10 normalized time, clipped between 0.15 and 3; curves without that crossing use 3. The architecture contains 14,765 trainable coefficients, including 488 embedding coefficients, for 1,359 training observations. Data-only learning uses the same architecture with kinetic, monotonicity, and initial-state penalties disabled.

Curve-balanced data loss and the total training objective are

[[EQ: L_{\mathrm{data}}=\frac{1}{61}\sum_i\frac{1}{N_{i,\mathrm{train}}}\sum_j(y_{\theta,ij}-y_{ij})^2. | 9a]]

[[EQ: L=L_{\mathrm{data}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{phys}}\langle\mathcal R_m^2\rangle+\lambda_{\mathrm{mono}}\langle[-\partial_\tau y_\theta]_+^2\rangle+\lambda_0\langle y_\theta(0)^2\rangle,\quad m\in\{\mathrm{FO},\mathrm{Av}\}. | 9b]]

The brackets denote averages over collocation points or initial states. Training used AdamW for 1,200 epochs, learning-rate decay from 0.002 to 0.0002, weight decay 10⁻⁶, gradient clipping at 5, λ_{mono}=0.02, and λ₀=0.05. Five nested-validation seeds selected λ_{phys}=3×10⁻⁴ from {3×10⁻⁵,10⁻⁴,3×10⁻⁴,10⁻³,3×10⁻³,10⁻²}. A separate 12-configuration search selected learning rate 0.004, 1,200 epochs, and weight decay 10⁻⁵ for the data-only network. Nested selection prevents terminal responses from serving as tuning targets [40]. Twenty matched seeds then quantified optimizer variability. The shape/initial-state ablation used the same learning rate, decay schedule, epochs, weight decay, collocation, λ_{mono}, λ₀, and seeds as the first-order PINNs, changing only λ_{phys} to zero. This isolates the residual at a fixed optimizer, but the separately tuned data-only comparison has unequal search budgets and does not isolate a physics effect. Avrami was evaluated at the common weight and independently tuned over the six positive weights plus zero. Because the data contain retained reversals, monotonicity is a soft assumption whose removal is tested separately. Configurations and software versions accompany the data.

### 2.4 Evaluation and Statistical Analysis

For each ordered curve, the final max{2,ceil(0.20N_i)} observations were withheld, producing 366 test and 1,359 training observations. The actual held-out fraction was 20 to 25%. Let e_{ijr} be prediction minus observation for curve i, test point j, and seed r. Curve-seed errors are

[[EQ: M_{ir}=N_{i,\mathrm{test}}^{-1}\sum_j|e_{ijr}|,\qquad Q_{ir}=\sqrt{N_{i,\mathrm{test}}^{-1}\sum_je_{ijr}^2}. | 10a]]

For R=20 optimization seeds, the seed-averaged curve error is

[[EQ: \bar M_i=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}M_{ir}. | 10b]]

The primary endpoint averages M̄_i equally over curves. For candidate c and comparator b, relative error reduction is

[[EQ: \rho_{cb}=100\frac{\sum_i(\bar M_{i,b}-\bar M_{i,c})}{\sum_i\bar M_{i,b}}. | 11]]

Observation-pooled MAE and RMSE instead weight each test point equally. We distinguish seed-mean errors from errors of the mean prediction: averaging forecasts before taking an absolute value is a different estimand. R² is secondary to absolute errors. A positive mean reduction need not imply that most curves improved.

All inference is exploratory: the reporting endpoint was chosen during development, not preregistered. Two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests use 61 paired, seed-averaged curve errors, not 20 seeds [41]. Holm correction covers 14 MAE/RMSE contrasts, including the fixed-optimizer zero-residual ablation [42]. Source dependence prevents these curve-level tests from establishing independent-study confirmation. We therefore emphasize exact source-sign enumeration and use 50,000 two-stage source/curve bootstrap replicates and source deletion descriptively [43]. With five sources, only 32 sign patterns exist and the minimum two-sided exact p value is 0.0625. A mixed-effects sensitivity models log curve MAE with a method effect and source and curve random intercepts; mixture-level clusters cannot be fitted because identifiers are incomplete. Sensitivities and strata remain exploratory and lie outside the 14-test family. Seeds measure optimization variation, not experimental replication.

Mean absolute scaled error (MASE) divides test MAE by the mean absolute consecutive training change [44,45]. It is descriptive because observation intervals are irregular. Values above one indicate errors larger than this in-sample change scale, not necessarily worse forecasts than held-out persistence. Post-selection conformal calculations are confined to the computational supplement because reused calibration residuals and source dependence do not justify prospective coverage guarantees [46]. Forecast-lead, truncation, embedding, gate-slope, and collocation sensitivities test specific failure modes; they do not turn the retrospective corpus into independent prospective validation.

## 3. Results and Discussion

### 3.1 Dataset and Direct Kinetic Fits

Figure 1 shows the heterogeneous expansion range and source composition. Kawabata and Brunetaud contribute 22 and 27 curves; the other studies contribute four each. Brunetaud supplies 55% of observations but 44% of curves, which explains why pooled and curve-balanced rankings can differ. Heat treatment spans 65 to 90 °C. Fourteen test segments increase by less than 0.01 percentage points, and seven decline from the final training measurement. Negligible (<0.04%), moderate (0.04 to <0.60%), and large (≥0.60%) terminal-response classes contain 15, 20, and 26 curves. These are descriptive strata, not durability thresholds, and their labels never enter training or selection.

Direct delayed first order achieved pooled MAE 0.0605% and RMSE 0.1380%. The empirical partially pooled comparator gave mean curve MAE 0.1406% and pooled MAE 0.1200%; its shrinkage did not improve this benchmark. It uses a hard-gate approximation and is not a controlled test of pooling alone. The other direct relations had larger pooled error tails (Fig. 2a). Their additional flexibility did not consistently improve extrapolation. Jacobian conditioning and bound contact in Fig. 2b indicate that a good trajectory fit need not uniquely determine its parameters. Direct Avrami fits contacted a bound on 45 curves. Long-term mortar observations also document the importance of delayed onset [47]. A fitted trajectory therefore does not establish uniquely recovered reaction constants, especially when much of the observed response precedes rapid expansion.

### 3.2 Neural Performance and Differential Compliance

Source-level evidence for improvement over data-only learning was weak: the exact sign-flip test gave p=0.4375. Table 2 separates optimizer variability from ensemble prediction errors. Delayed first-order PINNs achieved seed-averaged mean curve MAE of 0.0592%, compared with 0.0724% for separately tuned data-only learning (Fig. 3a,c). The 18.3% reduction survived curve-level Holm correction (p=0.000815; rank-biserial r_rb=0.567), but the descriptive source/curve bootstrap interval ranged from −3.0% to 33.0% (Table 3). Five source studies do not support a population-wide claim. The ensemble of PINNs achieved pooled MAE 0.0537%, RMSE 0.1065%, and R²=0.961. These ensemble values are distinct from the seed-mean estimand used in inference.

The fixed-optimizer ablation is more specific. Removing only the kinetic residual increased seed-averaged mean curve MAE from 0.0592% to 0.0774%, a 23.6% reduction for the residual-regularized model. The PINNs had lower MAE on 52 of 61 curves (Holm p=4.27×10⁻⁶; r_rb=0.747), and all five source-mean differences favored them. Nevertheless, the exact source-sign p value was 0.0625, its smallest possible two-sided value. A source/curve random-intercept sensitivity on log MAE reversed the average relative-error direction (log ratio 0.159, approximate p=0.023). That model is also exploratory with only five sources and no complete mixture identifiers. The arithmetic-mean gain thus depends on the error scale and is not a uniform proportional improvement.

Direct delayed first order had lower pooled MAE than the seed-mean PINNs (0.0605% versus 0.0633%). Seed-averaged curve errors favored the PINNs on 29 of 61 curves against direct fitting and 20 against persistence; neither contrast survived Holm correction (p=0.5530 and 0.4469). Persistence achieved pooled MAE 0.0607% and was more accurate on the remaining 41 curves. A few severe misses can favor the arithmetic mean while most curves favor a simpler method. For ensemble forecasts, median MASE was 2.76 and 26 curves had positive persistence-relative skill. Restricting direct-fit capacity, rate, and onset bounds to those of the neural head increased pooled MAE to 0.0799% (Table 2). This sensitivity still differs in objective (soft-L1 versus MSE) and model class: the neural state need not satisfy its head's trajectory or capacity. The broad-bound direct fit remains the primary comparator.

Differential compliance was weak. Across 20 seeds, median normalized residuals were 0.989 within the observed region and 0.975 in extrapolation. Median observed RMS derivative, kinetic right-hand side, and raw residual were 0.139, 0.0259, and 0.137, respectively, in normalized coordinates. The median per-curve right-hand-side/derivative RMS ratio was only 0.059. Thus, near-unit normalized violation chiefly reflects suppression of the kinetic right-hand side relative to the learned slope, not recovery of the governing equation. Curve-median normalized rates ranged from 0.128 to 0.589. Of 1,220 curve/seed rates, 838 lay within the lowest 1% of the allowed rate range; this wide-range criterion is not evidence of exact contact with the 0.02 bound. Capacity and onset showed no corresponding 1%-range contacts. These head values should not be interpreted as reaction constants.

The five-seed compliance sweep used {0,3×10⁻⁵,10⁻⁴,3×10⁻⁴,10⁻³,10⁻²,10⁻¹}, separately from the validation grid in Fig. 3b. Its observed normalized residual rose from 0.840 at 3×10⁻⁵ to 0.994 at 10⁻³, then fell to 0.543 at 0.1 (Fig. 3d). Meanwhile, the median raw residual fell from 0.196 to 0.105: normalization and changing right-hand-side magnitude explain why raw and normalized compliance need not move together. With zero residual weight, the normalized value was 0.972 but the raw residual was 1.191. At the selected weight, the five-seed normalized value was 0.980, distinct from the 20-seed value above. Pooled seed-mean terminal MAE varied little at the smaller positive weights and increased to 0.1087% at 0.1. Overlapping validation dispersions do not establish a uniquely optimal weight (Fig. 3b). Forecast accuracy therefore does not establish Eq. (3), crystallization mechanics, or pore-scale identification [7,48]. Adaptive balancing remains a possible investigation, not a demonstrated remedy [49].

Under the common first-order-selected physics weight, Avrami regularization degraded prediction. Its seed-averaged mean curve MAE was 0.1717%, approximately 137% above data-only learning; the Holm-adjusted p value was 3.19×10⁻⁵ and rank-biserial effect size was −0.686. Independent nested tuning also preferred zero penalty: validation mean curve MAE was 0.0862% without the residual and 0.1409 to 0.1458% for positive weights. The zero-weight objective reduces to the shape and initial-condition baseline in Table 2. Direct and neural Avrami exponents shared the same 1 to 4 support. These results do not invalidate Avrami kinetics in other experiments. They show that adding a plausible differential term can harm forecasts when the data do not identify its parameters and optimization does not enforce its dynamics.

### 3.3 Forecast Regimes and Robustness

Figure 4a shows late activation in Aubert C20 after a nearly flat training history, followed by 0.950% expansion. All displayed methods underestimated activation; the data-only ensemble was more accurate than the ensemble of PINNs (MAE 0.201% versus 0.309%). At the Kawabata 550 mm plateau (Fig. 4b), ensemble MAE was 0.0077% for the PINNs, 0.0819% for data-only learning, and 0.1237% for direct delayed first order; persistence gave 0.0443%. These forecasts were not equivalent. Tiecher CP II-Z was non-monotone during holdout, with a dip followed by recovery (Fig. 4c). The gray segment joins measurements and the colored markers identify predictions. The network already misses the final training points, so the forecast mismatch must not be interpreted as a discontinuous observation. Figure 4d includes all 366 held-out ensemble predictions without selecting only successful curves.

Performance depended on response magnitude. For large-response curves, ensemble MAE for the PINNs was 0.0723%, compared with 0.1363% for persistence and 0.1524% for direct delayed first order. For moderate and negligible responses, direct fitting achieved 0.0299% and 0.0022%, below the respective neural errors of 0.0693% and 0.0057%. At +90 d, supported by 32 curves, PINNs, persistence, and direct-fit MAE were 0.0580%, 0.0542%, and 0.0466%. Only four curves supported +365 d evaluation. These imbalances preclude a universal long-horizon ranking. The overall ensemble MAE of 0.0537% is about 537 microstrain, so a high pooled R² should not be mistaken for adequate accuracy near an engineering limit.

Five-seed sensitivities with gate slopes 40 and 100 or 9 and 36 collocation points gave pooled MAE between 0.0559% and 0.0564%. Collocation locations are resampled each epoch, so the 18-point spacing is not a permanently fixed unresolved grid; nevertheless, a narrow activation zone may be poorly sampled within an epoch. Additional checks restrict the domain to 0≤τ≤1.5 and concentrate half the points around the learned gate, with results retained in the computational supplement. Gate-aware sampling changes the residual-weighting measure and is not an otherwise identical quadrature rule. Removing the monotonicity penalty increased 20-seed mean curve MAE from 0.0592% to 0.0661%; it did not resolve the broader physical limitations. Scaling each curve by its own training maximum, with a 0.015% floor, reversed the five-seed neural ranking: mean curve MAE was 0.0554% for PINNs and 0.0498% for data-only learning. This sensitivity changes both relative loss weighting and the dimensional capacity support, so the original advantage cannot be called scaling-invariant.

Retaining only 75% or 50% of elapsed preterminal time increased mean curve MAE to 0.1276% and 0.2443%, compared with 0.1848% and 0.3210% for separately tuned data-only learning. These fractions refer to time, not point counts, and at least five training points were kept. At the same cuts, direct delayed first-order errors were 0.2137% and 0.2474%, and persistence errors were 0.1775% and 0.3254%. The complete nine-comparator table accompanies the analysis files. The very small difference between PINNs and direct first order at the earlier cut does not establish a general neural advantage. A complete onset-stratified rolling-origin benchmark remains necessary before claiming robustness to activation timing. The deliberate full-series-scaling sensitivity did not improve the primary result and retained the neural ordering. These retrospective checks do not provide prospective uncertainty guarantees or replace a DEF performance test [50,51].

### 3.4 Materials Interpretation and Limits

The model describes effective DEF expansion kinetics rather than an elementary reaction mechanism. The reaction balance defines the chemical inventory, while the observation operator distinguishes product volume from measured strain. Capacity and onset combine reactive inventory, moisture access, accommodation, damage, and restraint. If the preactivation rate differs from the visible rate, even the conditional threshold ratio changes. The available records cannot calibrate transport or crystallization-pressure fields. The reduced first-order law instead permits a direct test of whether a kinetic restriction improves the continuation of a measured expansion history.

Known material controls remain important but are insufficiently resolved here. SCMs change sulfate binding and microstructure [52,53]. Fly ash modifies heat-cured mortar response [54]. Aggregate and recycled constituents affect phase assemblage and strain transfer [55,56]. Chlorides can alter delayed expansion [57,58]. Moisture cycling changes the measured trajectory [33,59]. Restraint modifies both expansion and damage [60,61]. Static descriptors and missingness flags cannot represent these evolving processes. In the source order Brunetaud, Tiecher, Aubert, Martin, and Kawabata, descriptor-only leave-one-source-out PINNs had seed-mean pooled MAE of 0.130%, 0.402%, 1.010%, 0.563%, and 0.994%; data-only errors were 0.265%, 0.513%, 0.980%, 0.575%, and 0.993%. Thus the PINNs improved three of five sources, but transfer errors remained large. Hyperparameters were selected from the overall corpus rather than nested within each source holdout, so this is an exploratory transfer diagnostic, not independent external validation.

The evidence also remains limited by lawful-access and vector-figure availability, one-analyst screening, no blinded repeat digitization, and source imbalance. The extraction filter removed ambiguous branches rather than resolving them experimentally. Clipped negative training values may affect learned weights even though terminal targets were unchanged. Curves within a study share materials and protocols; curve-level significance is not study-level confirmation. A prospective benchmark needs replicate specimens with aligned expansion, thermal and moisture histories, phase inventories, porosity, and restraint. Until then, the model should support comparison of candidate continuations, not pass/fail decisions, unseen-mixture screening, or structural safety assessment.

## 4. Conclusions

1. A balanced chemical inventory and a linear observation model distinguish DEF conversion from measured expansion. The fitted capacity, rate, and onset are effective trajectory parameters, not independently identified chemical constants.
2. Source-level evidence for an improvement over data-only learning was weak (exact p=0.4375). Delayed first-order PINNs gave seed-averaged mean curve MAE of 0.0592% versus 0.0724% for separately tuned data-only learning and 0.0774% for the fixed-optimizer zero-residual ablation. Curve-level contrasts do not establish source-wide or uniform proportional improvement.
3. Direct kinetics retained lower pooled MAE than the seed-mean PINNs, and persistence was more accurate on most curves. Avrami regularization degraded prediction, and per-curve scaling reversed the neural ranking. Normalized residuals near unity limit the result to kinetic regularization rather than governing-equation recovery.
4. The validated task is terminal forecasting of observed series. Sparse chemistry and weak source transfer do not support susceptibility prediction for unseen cementitious systems.

## Appendix A. Mathematical and Reproducibility Checks

The clock in Eq. (4) starts at zero and has derivative H_s. Applying the chain rule to Eq. (5) recovers Eq. (3). Likewise, writing u=κΩ_s in Eq. (6a) gives

[[EQ: \partial_\tau y=H_s n\kappa(\kappa\Omega_s)^{n-1}(\alpha-y), | A1]]

which is the zero-residual form of Eq. (8b). For n=1 it reduces to delayed first order. In the limit s→∞, Ω_s→[τ−τ₀]₊. These identities verify the implemented observable kinetics without asserting that a nonzero-penalty neural solution satisfies them exactly. Units also close: k is inverse time, κ and τ are dimensionless, and strain normalized by E* is dimensionless. Chemical component balances in Eq. (1) and the anhydrite route were checked separately.

For the paired inference, d_i=M̄_{i,b}−M̄_{i,c} and r_rb=(W₊−W₋)/(W₊+W₋), excluding zero differences. Ordering the 14 raw p values by index h gives

[[EQ: p^{\mathrm{Holm}}_{(h)}=\max_{\ell\leq h}\{\min[1,(15-\ell)p_{(\ell)}]\}. | A2]]

The hierarchical bootstrap samples five source labels with replacement and then curves within each selected source. The mean over all sampled curves estimates the curve-weighted contrast, not an equal-source estimand. Exact source-sign enumeration and source deletion diagnose the limited independent evidence.

Reproduction checks covered input hashes, source-coordinate reconstruction, split membership, training-only scales, unique prediction keys, finite outputs, and independent metric recalculation. Re-extraction matched the archived dataset hash exactly, which checks implementation repeatability rather than independent digitization accuracy. Symbolic identities and 27 numerical integrations verified the reduced equations, with maximum state error below 1.5×10⁻¹⁰. Neural comparisons retain separate seed-level and ensemble-level estimands. Conformal calculations are documented only in the computational supplement because this design does not justify prospective coverage guarantees.

For the gypsum route, the solid-volume increment is the ettringite molar volume minus the monosulfate molar volume and twice the gypsum molar volume. The total reaction-volume change also subtracts 16 molar volumes of consumed water; the anhydrite route subtracts 20. Consequently, a positive solid-product increment need not imply a positive total-volume change. Converting either quantity to concrete strain would additionally require reactive inventory, pore accommodation, localization, and restraint. Those quantities were not measured jointly here, so numerical mineral-volume ratios are not used to constrain the model or infer specimen expansion.
